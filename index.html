1.1 · What Is the Environment, and What Is an Ecosystem?

Environment, Systems & Ecosystems

Environment
All living and nonliving things on Earth that sustain life — an interconnected collection of systems.
System
Interacting components that produce patterns of behavior over time (e.g., a household or a watershed).
Ecosystem
Natural systems within the environment — such as a coral reef — that cycle nutrients and support life.

Ecosystem services: humans rely on natural systems for oxygen, clean water, climate regulation, and nutrient cycling.

© 2023 W. W. Norton & Company, Inc.
1.1  ·  What Is an Ecosystem?

Ecosystems

  • Ecosystems combine biotic (living) and abiotic (nonliving) elements.
  • Everything is interconnected: organisms, water, soil, air, and energy all interact.
  • Terrestrial: forest, desert, tundra, grasslands. Aquatic: marine and freshwater.
Ecosystems Are Dynamic
  • Web of influence: a change in one part of an ecosystem ripples outward to others.
  • Cascading consequences: small disturbances can trigger large, unexpected effects.
  • Spannocchia: the olive fruit fly (Bactrocera oleae) destroyed the entire 2014 olive crop, one pest cascading through a managed ecosystem.

1.2  ·  What Is Sustainability?

The Triple Bottom Line

UN definition (1987): “Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.”

Planet Environmental
Ecosystem health and environmental protection.
People Social
Human well-being and equity.
Profit Economic
Economic viability and prosperity.

Sustainability lives where all three overlap — hover a card to find it on the diagram. Fall short on any one dimension and the whole thing stops being sustainable.

1.2 · What Is Sustainability?

The UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

In 2015, all 193 UN member states adopted the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development — putting the triple bottom line to work as 17 shared global goals.

  • 17 goals and 169 targets, with a target date of 2030.
  • They span all three dimensions: environmental (13 Climate Action), social (5 Gender Equality), and economic (8 Decent Work).
  • Integrated and indivisible — progress on one goal depends on the others, and they can pull against each other.

United Nations (2015). Transforming Our World: The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, A/RES/70/1.

1.2 · Ecological Resilience

What Is Ecological Resilience?

  • Healthy ecosystems can withstand both natural and human-caused disruptions.
  • Ecological resilience measures how well a system recovers after a disturbance.
  • Coastal cities like New York are restoring shoreline ecosystems after Superstorm Sandy to buffer future storms.
  • Sustainable management maintains the resilience that allows ecosystem services to continue.

1.3 · What Is Environmental Justice?

Environmental Justice (EJ)

EJ raises awareness that marginalized communities receive more of the harms of resource extraction and fewer of the benefits — such as clean air — and seeks to right these injustices.

Equity as a Core Value
One of the 3Es of sustainability — fairness to all groups of people, now and in the future.
Structural Disparities
Poor and marginalized communities disproportionately bear the costs of resource extraction and industrial land use.
Community Inclusion
Transparent government and industry processes must include affected communities in decisions.
Global Concern
Social unrest arises when environmental harms fall unevenly — EJ is a worldwide challenge.

1.4 · What Is Science?

Science as a Process

1 · Observe
Identify a natural phenomenon or problem.
2 · Question
Frame a testable scientific question.
3 · Hypothesize
Propose an explanation or predicted answer.
4 · Experiment
Design a controlled test to gather evidence.
5 · Analyze & Report
Interpret results and share them with the scientific community.

1.4 · Observation, Hypothesis & Controlled Experiments

Probabilistic Models

Probabilistic models account for uncertainty: they give the probability of possible outcomes rather than a single fixed value.

  • Weather forecasts.
  • Climate projections.
  • Election forecasts.

Easy to misinterpret! A 70% chance of rain doesn't mean it will definitely rain — it means that 30% of the time, it shouldn't.

1.5 · What Are Challenges to Good Science?

Fraud, Pseudoscience, Bias & Misinformation

Fraud
Intentional deception — presenting false findings as fact. Damages scientific trust and can mislead policy.
Pseudoscience
Claims that appear scientific but lack proper methodology. Not necessarily deceptive, but misleading.
Bias
Unreasonable weighting of sources, or prejudices, that lead to errors and distort the interpretation of evidence.
Misinformation
False or inaccurate information — spread intentionally or not — that shapes public understanding.

Peer review is the scientific community's main safeguard: experts assess whether a study's design and conclusions hold up before publication.

1.6 · What Shapes Our Decisions on the Environment?

Values, Trade-Offs & Incentives

Values
Science identifies what is; values determine what should be. Environmental decisions require both.
Trade-Offs
Weighing the benefits vs. costs of alternative actions — a core part of any policy or personal decision.
Incentives
Positive or negative signals (taxes, subsidies, labels) can shift behavior at scale.

1.X · Key Concepts in SETs

Key Concepts in SETs

Boundaries
The limits of a system, separating it from its environment by specifying what is included and excluded from consideration.
Feedback
It alters the rate of change in a system. Positive feedback amplifies the change, while negative feedback abates the change.
Tipping point
A critical threshold where accumulated changes trigger a drastic shift into a different and often irreversible stable state.
Resilience
The tendency of a system to return to or remain within stable conditions, despite a change in forces acting upon the system
Emergent Property
A characteristic resulting from interactions among parts of a system, not individual components.
